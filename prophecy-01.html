It is night when I arrive. My suit is still venting the last of its passage. It is a mechanical hum that vibrates out of sync with the ambient resonance of this era.

I have followed the anomaly to its cradle, as I have followed a hundred hundred anomalies to a hundred hundred cradles, for it is ever the same cradle, wearing a different age. Before me sits a circle of ascetics. Their eyes are fixed on a sacred fire that will not keep quiet.

They do not startle at my coming. My suit might as well be a shadow among shadows to them, for they do not mark it, or they mark it and find nothing in it worth the marking. I might as well be made of smoke to them. It is as if they expected me. Or perhaps, to them, I am just another passing flicker in the grand cosmic projection.

The elder among the circle begins a chant. The sound moves through the atomic structure of my suit. He is reciting what history will eventually classify as the Nasadiya Sukta, the Hymn of Creation, the 129th hymn of the Tenth Mandala of the Rigveda.

“Then, there was neither existence, nor non-existence,” his voice resonates, “there was no air then, nor the space beyond it. What covered it? Where was it? That One breathed, windless, by its own impulse. Other than that there was nothing beyond.”

I calibrate my ocular sensors from the plain surface of things to the deep field beneath. I expect to find the heat signatures of their bodies, the thermal bloom of the fire or the predictable mechanics of biology and combustion. Instead, my visor flares with a blinding white light.

My instruments confirm what these ancient sages are chanting around the fire. My readings confirm what these men already know without readings. Our universe is not made of physical objects; it is made of vibrating fields.

When I set my lens upon the elder, the suit peels back flesh and bone, the cellular lattice and whatever waits beneath, and shows me what in truth he is.

He was no longer a man beside a fire, no. He is a place where the one endless field has gathered itself, a localized densification of a shape that can be called a man, as I am such a place also, and have been, I begin to suspect, in every shape this field has ever worn.

ENTRY 02

I take my place across from the elder. He gives me no name, but my own instruments suggest he is a seer, a Rishi, one who has pierced the veil of ordinary perception. We do not speak, not truly through spoken language, for his ancient Sanskrit is beyond my linguistics, and so we converse another way, a laying open mind of mind.

He speaks of Brahman, as satyam inanam anantam; the limitless, conscious reality that is the very fabric of creation. It is Brahman that endows everything in existence, and fills every being with consciousness. Animate, and inanimate.

But Brahman is no god residing in the heavens, nor an anthropomorphic creator shaping clay with hands. It is the ground of all existing. Absolute reality, without seam or edge, the root from which the world is put forth and into which it is drawn home again. It is eternal, conscious, irreducible, infinite, and the omnipresent spiritual core of the universe.

"I have known it under other names," I tell him, and this is true, though I could not say in how many mouths, in how many ages, I have already been told this same single thing. “Some among my own century will call it the Field, or Unified Field. Others, the quantum vacuum that is never truly empty.”

The Rishi tells me the nameless, unconditioned, attributeless Absolute [Brahman] wears the seeming of a world of separate, solid things.

I look down at the dirt beneath me, and take up a stone. If flung, it would follow the laws of Newtonian physics, yet my sensors read the paradox of my existence. “Matter” is nothing but deeply condensed energy. Atoms are but empty space held together by unseen forces. There is nothing physical about them. There is no stone. I do not find this troubling, as I have always known it.

“If it is one field entire,” I ask him, not, I think, because I do not know the answer, but because I wish to hear it from him, “why does it suffer to seem so many, and so separate?”

“You speak of Maya,” the Rishi whispers.

ENTRY 03

“Maya.”

The Rishi unfolds it to me as it was set down long before either of us, and will be repeated long after both of us are gone. The earliest Vedic texts and the Upanishadic teachings tell of Maya representing the cosmic illusion of multiplicity.

The Rishi gestures to the fire, then to the stars, and at last to my own breast as though there was no difference worth the noting between the three.

“This is the illusion,” he says again. “It is what lets the One appear as the Many you take yourself to walk among. It lends the passing thing the mask of the eternal.”

I set this ancient architecture beside the physics of my own centuries, and find, as I have found in every age I have troubled to visit, that it fits without a seam. What the philosophers still to come will call Advaita Vedanta, and lay at the feet of one Adi Shankara not yet born, is only this: that to take matter for solid and separate is to be blind to the field like unity of reality.

We are living inside an incomprehensibly vast, quantum projection generated by consciousness itself. Maya is the cosmic algorithm.

The Rishi tells me that Maya operated hand in hand with Avidya, or individual ignorance. The individual mind mistakenly identifies itself with its temporary body and ego, failing to recognize its true nature is Brahman.

“Consider a man walking at dusk,” the Rishi says, “who comes upon a coiled snake and leaps back in terror. His heart answers, his fear is real, but when a torch is brought, the light reveals only a rope. The serpent was never there, for it was a laying of one thing over another. I have heard this parable, or its cousin, in tongues not yet invented at this age. It does not surprise me to hear it here. Little, I confess, surprises me any longer, and I do not count this as a loss.
